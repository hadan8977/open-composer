                                                                                  Abstract

                                               Sparsity or complexity? In modern high-dimensional asset pricing, these are of-

                                            ten viewed as competing principles: recent empirical evidence favors richer models,

                                            while economic intuition has long favored parsimony. We reconcile this tension by

                                            distinguishing capacity sparsity—restrictions on effective model capacity—from factor

                                            sparsity—the parsimonious structure of priced risks. Revisiting the benchmark empir-

                                            ical design of Didisheim et al. (2025), we combine nonlinear feature expansions with

                                            basis pursuit, using column generation and GPU acceleration to scale estimation to

                                            432 million candidate factors. Reaching this scale reveals a reversal in out-of-sample

                                            performance: sparse portfolios trail dense ridgeless benchmarks at lower complexity

                                            but achieve a higher Sharpe ratio and lower pricing error at the largest candidate set.

                                            Capacity expansion and factor sparsity are therefore complements: enlarging the can-

                                            didate space allows a parsimonious pricing kernel to outperform its dense counterpart.

                                            Keywords: Asset pricing, stochastic discount factor, factor models, overparameteri-

                                            zation, basis pursuit, machine learning
1     Introduction

Recent work (Kelly et al., 2024; Didisheim et al., 2025; Kelly and Malamud, 2025) highlights
a virtue of complexity in empirical asset pricing: large nonlinear factor sets, when com-
bined with shrinkage, can outperform traditional parsimonious models. As high-dimensional
methods become central to machine-learning-based SDF estimation, sparsity—long a guid-
ing principle in empirical asset pricing and econometrics—appears increasingly marginalized.
The prevailing message is that richer models dominate simpler ones.
    Didisheim et al. (2025) illustrate this shift by comparing models built from large factor
sets with (i) traditional low-dimensional factor models and (ii) models that compress a large
set of factors into a few principal components (Kozak et al., 2020). They show that high-
dimensional specifications outperform both alternatives and argue that principal-component
compression may discard low-variance directions that still contain economically meaningful
pricing information. Yet the idea that “more is better” remains difficult to reconcile with
the long-standing economic discipline of parsimony in asset pricing. While large factor sets
may improve performance, it remains unclear what economic structure an ever-expanding
set of factors actually captures.
    We reconcile this tension by distinguishing two notions of sparsity: capacity sparsity and
factor sparsity. The former concerns restrictions on effective model capacity, whereas the lat-
ter refers to the parsimonious economic structure of priced risks. In Didisheim et al. (2025),
the sparsity at issue pertains primarily to capacity sparsity, as their critique is directed at the
underperformance of approaches that restrict effective model capacity, whether through clas-
sical low-dimensional asset-pricing structures, dimension reduction such as PCA, or strong
shrinkage imposed on a rich set of candidate factors. Our key insight is that capacity ex-
pansion and factor sparsity are complements. Complexity expands the menu of candidate
components, while sparsity determines which components are selected. A richer candidate
space can therefore improve the performance of a pricing kernel with few active factors.



                                                1
   To operationalize this idea, we estimate conditional stochastic discount factors (SDFs)
using a rich nonlinear feature expansion together with basis pursuit, the minimum-ℓ1 in-
terpolating solution. The feature expansion enlarges the candidate factor span, while basis
pursuit selects a parsimonious pricing kernel within that span. In contrast to approaches
that discipline complexity by compressing the candidate space itself, our approach retains
high capacity and imposes parsimony at the level of the selected pricing kernel.
   To examine the empirical value of factor sparsity, we adopt the “apples-to-apples” em-
pirical design of Didisheim et al. (2025). Holding the information set fixed, we vary model
complexity by expanding the number of nonlinear factors P . A key property of basis pursuit
is that it admits an optimal solution with at most T active factors, where T denotes the
number of in-sample observations. Consequently, even as the candidate feature space be-
comes highly overparameterized—particularly in regimes where P ≫ T —the resulting SDF
remains sparse relative to the large factor models considered in Didisheim et al. (2025).
   Our main specification uses a rolling training window of T = 180 months and expands the
candidate set to 432 million factors, or a complexity ratio of c = P/T = 2.4 million. Basis
pursuit admits an optimal solution with at most 180 active factors. We make estimation
feasible at this scale by combining column generation with GPU acceleration. At lower
complexity, the dense SDF outperforms the sparse SDF. As complexity increases, dense
performance largely plateaus, while sparse performance improves overall. At the largest
candidate set, the annualized Sharpe ratios are approximately 3.59 for the sparse SDF and
3.53 for the dense benchmark, and the sparse SDF also attains a lower pricing error. The
endpoint advantage is modest, but the reversal in ranking is consequential: it occurs well
beyond the upper complexity ratio of c = 1,000 examined by Didisheim et al. (2025). A
comparison confined to that range would miss the performance attainable by a sparse pricing
kernel selected from a much larger candidate space.
   The return decomposition helps explain this reversal. At high complexity, the dense
SDF’s mean return and volatility largely stabilize, while the sparse SDF benefits from both


                                             2
higher mean returns and further volatility reduction as the candidate set expands. At the
largest candidate set, the sparse portfolio earns a higher mean return and a higher Sharpe ra-
tio despite remaining somewhat more volatile than the dense benchmark. It nearly dominates
across the four reported return quantiles: its 25th, 75th, and 90th percentiles are higher,
while its 10th percentile remains slightly lower. After scaling both portfolios to a common
annualized volatility of 10%, the sparse SDF also delivers a higher certainty-equivalent re-
turn at the largest candidate set for each of the three risk-aversion coefficients examined,
γ ∈ {1, 2, 5}. The advantage of sparse selection thus survives a utility-based comparison at
common volatility.
   These results reframe the virtue-of-complexity debate. A large candidate factor space
can support an effective pricing kernel with few active factors. Factor sparsity therefore
remains an economically useful principle in high-capacity asset pricing.


1.1    Related Literature

Our paper contributes to the growing literature that integrates high-capacity machine learn-
ing methods into empirical asset pricing. Within this literature, two closely related streams
have emerged. The first focuses on improving return prediction from large sets of firm
characteristics using flexible nonlinear prediction rules and extensive model comparisons.
This work primarily addresses the forecasting problem at the level of individual assets or
characteristic-sorted portfolios (e.g., Gu et al., 2020; see also Feng et al., 2020 for evidence
on disciplined factor selection in large factor collections). A second stream applies machine
learning methods to estimate conditional stochastic discount factors (SDFs) or conditional
factor structures that price the cross-section of assets. Representative examples include
deep-learning approaches that impose no-arbitrage moment restrictions in the estimation
of the pricing kernel (e.g., Chen et al., 2024) and latent-factor models with conditioning
information embedded through modern dimension-reduction architectures (e.g., Kelly et al.,
2019; Gu et al., 2021). Our paper falls within this second strand. We study conditional


                                               3
SDF estimation in high-capacity settings and examine how model complexity translates into
economic performance.
   Our work also contributes to the recent debate on the virtue of complexity in empir-
ical asset pricing. Recent evidence suggests that increasingly complex models—including
overparameterized feature expansions paired with shrinkage—can deliver large gains in out-
of-sample performance in return prediction and in factor pricing models (e.g., Kelly et al.,
2024; Didisheim et al., 2025). At the same time, recent critiques argue that some apparent
gains from complexity in return prediction can reflect statistical artifacts or unstable extrap-
olation in highly flexible specifications (e.g., Nagel, 2025). Related work clarifies why such
critiques need not overturn the core virtue-of-complexity evidence, while also highlighting
that complex models can exhibit nuanced behavior that depends on design and estimator
choice (e.g., Kelly and Malamud, 2025). Our results complement this debate by sharpening
the mechanism: when the hypothesis class is rich, economic performance depends not only
on the availability of many candidate components, but also on the selection principle that
maps complexity into factor exposures.
   More broadly, our paper relates to the long-standing role of sparsity and parsimony in em-
pirical asset pricing. Classical practice emphasizes small factor representations, while mod-
ern high-dimensional approaches often discipline estimation through shrinkage and eigen-
structure-aware regularization. For example, Kozak et al. (2020) construct robust SDFs in
large candidate sets by shrinking exposures associated with low-variance principal-component
directions. In contrast, recent virtue-of-complexity evidence argues that aggressively com-
pressing large factor sets through dimension reduction can discard economically meaning-
ful low-variance directions and thereby degrade pricing performance (e.g., Didisheim et al.,
2025). Our analysis clarifies that these perspectives concern different notions of sparsity. We
distinguish capacity sparsity, which restricts effective model capacity, from factor sparsity,
which concerns whether the underlying economic structure of priced risks remains parsimo-
nious. This distinction allows complexity and sparsity to coexist: complexity expands the


                                               4
hypothesis class, while sparsity governs selection of economically meaningful components
within it.
    This perspective also connects to insights from statistical learning theory that emphasize
the role of inductive bias in overparameterized regimes. When many solutions fit the data
similarly well, generalization is governed not only by model capacity but also by the esti-
mator’s implicit preferences over the solution set. Wilson (2025) develops this idea through
the lens of soft inductive bias, and related work on interpolation, double descent, and benign
overfitting shows how selection rules within high-capacity classes can produce counterintu-
itive out-of-sample behavior even when training error is driven to zero (e.g., Hastie et al.,
2022; Bartlett et al., 2020). Applied to asset pricing, the relevant question is therefore not
only whether the SDF is estimated in a high-dimensional space, but which pricing kernel is
selected from a large set of near-equivalent high-dimensional solutions.
    Finally, our findings provide a complementary perspective on the “factor zoo” in empirical
asset pricing. The proliferation of proposed factors has long been noted (e.g., Cochrane, 2011)
and is closely tied to concerns about multiple testing, replication, and post-publication decay
in characteristic-based predictors (e.g., Harvey et al., 2016; Hou et al., 2020; McLean and
Pontiff, 2016). At the same time, modern open-data efforts document broad reproducibility
of many published predictors when construction choices are standardized (e.g., Chen and
Zimmermann, 2022; Jensen et al., 2023). Our results suggest an additional interpretation
that is especially salient in high-capacity conditional SDF settings: the challenge is not
merely that many candidate factors exist, but that identifying the economically relevant
ones requires a powerful search space combined with an effective selection mechanism.



2     Empirical Methodology

This section develops the empirical framework for estimating conditional stochastic discount
factors (SDFs) and introduces the two notions of sparsity that arise in high-capacity asset

increasing model complexity can help reveal sparse economic structure. Next, we present our
estimation approach, which combines scalable nonlinear feature expansions with basis pursuit
to identify sparse pricing kernels within a large factor span. Finally, we characterize sparse
and dense interpolating SDFs, clarifying when and why sparse selection can outperform
ridgeless estimation.

2.2       Two Notions of Sparsity

Recent work on the “virtue of complexity” (Didisheim et al., 2025) studies regimes in which
the number of candidate factors P is large relative to the time-series sample size T . In such
settings, estimation is often stabilized through ridge regularization,

       For γ > 0, this formulation introduces ℓ2 -shrinkage into the estimator. For non-negligible
values of γ, the shrinkage acts as a form of capacity control by disciplining estimation in a
large candidate factor space. We refer to this broader idea as capacity sparsity. It can also
arise through ex ante restrictions on the factor span (for example, selecting a small number
of economically motivated factors), through dimension-reduction methods such as principal
components, or through shrinkage more broadly.
       By contrast, our focus is on a different notion of sparsity, which we call factor sparsity.
Rather than restricting the size of the factor span, factor sparsity concerns the structure of
the pricing kernel itself. Under this view, the true SDF may depend on only a small subset
of economically meaningful directions, even when the candidate factor space is large. The
key difficulty, however, is that this sparse structure is unknown ex ante. Precisely for that
reason, increasing model complexity can play a constructive role. By enlarging the space
of candidate factors, richer feature representations expand the set of directions in which
the true sparse structure may lie. Complexity therefore facilitates discovery, while sparsity
governs selection within the enlarged space of candidate pricing kernels. Figure 1 provides a
schematic illustration of these two notions of sparsity and their interplay. In the figure, the

candidate space can support higher mean returns without an offsetting increase in volatility,
providing a possible explanation for the empirical improvement in sparse SDF Sharpe ratios.
    The economic implication is that exact in-sample fit leaves room for selection to matter
out of sample. Lemma 1 shows that expanding the candidate set need not increase the
support bound of the selected kernel; Proposition 1 identifies the alignment needed for a
sparse interpolator to earn a higher mean return. The empirical analysis asks whether a
sparse representation can realize this advantage as complexity increases.

4     Conclusion

This paper studies how complexity and parsimony interact in empirical asset pricing. We
argue that the apparent tension between them reflects a distinction between two different
notions of sparsity: capacity sparsity, which restricts effective model capacity, and factor
sparsity, which concerns the parsimonious structure of priced risks within an expanded
candidate space. Our results support the view that richer candidate spaces can improve
asset-pricing performance by creating more scope for informative sparse selection.
    Empirically, we show that when the information set is held fixed and model complexity
is increased through richer nonlinear feature expansions, sparse and dense selection produce
distinct performance paths. The sparse SDF selected by basis pursuit improves overall in the
high-complexity regime, whereas the dense ridgeless benchmark plateaus. Our scalable im-
plementation of basis pursuit reaches the complexity range in which the relative performance
of sparse and dense SDFs reverses. The computational extension changes the empirical as-
sessment of sparsity: a comparison confined to lower complexity would miss the performance
attainable by a sparse pricing kernel drawn from a much larger candidate space. The sparse
SDF nearly dominates across the four reported return quantiles at the largest candidate set,
with a small remaining shortfall at the 10th percentile. At a common annualized volatility
of 10%, it also delivers a higher CE for each of the three risk-aversion coefficients examined.
These findings support the economic value of a sparse factor representation.
    Our analysis helps clarify why this pattern can arise. In overparameterized settings where
many pricing kernels fit the data equally well in sample, performance depends not only on



                                              28
the richness of the factor space but also on the selection rule used to choose among admissible
kernels. Sparse selection can outperform dense interpolation when it moves toward econom-
ically relevant directions that the minimum-ℓ2 rule does not exploit, and that advantage can
become larger as complexity increases.
   Taken together, our results reframe the virtue-of-complexity debate. A richer candidate
space can improve the performance of a pricing kernel with few active factors. Capacity
expansion and factor sparsity can therefore work together in empirical asset pricing.
