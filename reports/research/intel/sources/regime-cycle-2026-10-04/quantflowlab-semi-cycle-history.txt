 Semiconductor Cycle History: SOX Drawdowns, Recoveries, and Triggers
 By Aiden Min · March 28, 2026 · Updated August 4, 2026 

 🔄 Updated August 4, 2026 
 Between September 2004 and August 2026, the SOX index recorded seven completed declines of at least 20% from a closing high. The shallowest ran −25.05% . The deepest ran −68.66% . Observed recoveries ran from under three months to more than five years.
 I rebuilt this semiconductor cycle history from a single daily closing-price series rather than assembling episodes from secondary accounts. That changed the answer. Two declines I had previously omitted — 2006 and 2015–16 — qualify, and the 2024–25 drawdown turns out to be the third-deepest qualifying episode in this dataset.
 This is not a case for optimism or pessimism. It is a record of what the series shows, an explicit statement of the rule used to identify episodes, and a separate accounting of the decline still underway as of the August 3, 2026 cutoff.
 For the dollar-level capex breakdown, see AI Semiconductor Spending . For ETF-level return history and leverage mechanics, see Semiconductor Downturn History .

 §1 — The Computed Record Seven Completed Drawdown Episodes
 The PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index, known as SOX, is the benchmark used throughout. Nasdaq’s index materials record that it began on December 1, 1993 at a base value of 200.00 and split two-for-one in July 1995.
 It holds 30 companies under a capping rule limiting the largest constituent to 12%, the second to 10%, the third to 8%, and the rest to 4% each, per the index methodology document .
 Episode Rule Used
 Source series: Nasdaq daily closing values for SOX as published via FRED series NASDAQSOX , covering September 2, 2004 through August 3, 2026 
 An episode begins at a new running closing high within that series
 The trough is the lowest subsequent observed close before recovery
 Recovery is the first closing date at or above the prior peak
 Included: completed episodes with a peak-to-trough decline of at least 20%
 Episodes are non-overlapping and are sorted deepest to shallowest
 Formula: decline = (trough close ÷ peak close) − 1
 These are the completed qualifying episodes identified within the available post-September-2004 series. They are not a list of all semiconductor cycles, all SOX declines, or the largest drawdowns over thirty years.

 Data Limitation — Episode 1
 The series carries no observations from May 20, 2008 through October 31, 2008 . Episode 1 is therefore based on available closing observations rather than an uninterrupted daily path.
 The observed July 17, 2007 peak of 546.59 and the November 20, 2008 low of 171.32 produce a −68.66% decline, but the intervening gap means neither the exact trough nor the trough-to-recovery duration can be verified from this series alone. The other six episodes fall entirely outside the gap. Not all seven are verified to the same standard.

 Two features stand out. Depth and recovery speed are only loosely related: the 2024–25 decline was the third-deepest qualifying episode in this dataset yet recovered in 5.1 months, while the shallower 2006 episode took 11.9 months. And declines of this size are not rare within this window — seven in under twenty-two years.
 §2 — Anatomy Different Triggers, One Mechanism
 Across this semiconductor cycle history the catalysts differ, but the mechanism underneath does not: a new fabrication plant costs tens of billions and takes years to reach volume, so supply is inelastic in the short run. Ordinary swings in demand become large swings in price.
 2007–08 was a systemic credit crisis rather than a chip-specific one, and it produced both the deepest decline and by far the slowest recovery on this record — more than five years back to the prior peak.
 2021–22 followed pandemic-era inventory accumulation, met rising interest rates and cooling consumer electronics demand, and recovered as generative AI emerged as a new demand driver.
 2024–25 ran −39.66% over roughly nine months and recovered in about five. It is the third-deepest qualifying episode in this dataset, and the fastest recovery among the three deepest.
 2020 was the sharpest and shortest: a 28-day decline, back to the prior peak within 2.6 months, after which the pandemic turned into an accelerant for chip demand.
 2006, 2015–16 and 2018 were shallower and more conventional: inventory corrections and, in 2018, tariff escalation and export-control uncertainty affecting companies with significant China revenue.
 Historical Context: The 2000–2002 Dot-Com Bust
 The dot-com collapse remains the reference point most readers arrive with, and it belongs in any account of the sector. It is deliberately not in the table above: it falls entirely outside the Nasdaq/FRED series used for that computation, which begins in September 2004.
 Internet infrastructure and telecom build-out drove chip demand through the late 1990s. When that spending reversed, capacity added during the boom arrived into collapsing orders. StockCharts data cited in April 2026 places the index near 1,362 in March 2000 and near 209 by late 2002 — a decline of more than four-fifths.
 Those are approximate index levels, not verified closing values with dates, so no precise percentage is stated here and the episode is excluded from the ranking, the decline range, the recovery statistics and the cards above.
 §3 — The Revenue Base Repeated Declines, a Rising Long-Run Trend
 Industry revenue has fallen in individual years — 2001, 2009, 2019 and 2023 among them. What has risen is the long-run trend. Global semiconductor sales grew from $139 billion in 2001 to roughly $574 billion in 2022. SIA’s July 2026 industry report lists revised 2025 global semiconductor sales at $795.6 billion .
 The WSTS Spring 2026 forecast projects approximately $1.51 trillion for 2026 and $1.91 trillion for 2027. Those are forecasts, revised semi-annually, not realized revenue.
 Each demand driver — PCs, phones, cloud, now AI — lifted the baseline. The declines did not stop. They occurred against a base that kept moving up over multi-year spans.

 §4 — Geographic Concentration Where Production Now Sits
 In the 1980s Japan was the center of gravity, and that era’s boom-bust dynamics ran through Japanese capacity investment and U.S.–Japan trade friction. The center has moved to Taiwan and South Korea.
 Advanced logic manufacturing is heavily concentrated in TSMC. In memory, the clearest figures available come from an issuer filing.
 SK hynix’s July 2026 SEC filing cites IDC estimates showing the company first globally in high-bandwidth memory at 56.4% revenue share in Q1 2026, second in DRAM including HBM at 29.1% , and second in NAND at 18.5%.
 These are third-party estimates reproduced in a filing, not the issuer’s own measurements.

 The CHIPS Act and comparable programs in Europe and Japan aim to diversify manufacturing, but new fabs take years to reach volume production.
 §5 — The AI Cycle What’s Different, What Isn’t
 The current boom has features earlier episodes lacked, and features that preceded several of them. Both readings survive contact with the data.
 Different: procurement structure and physical bottlenecks. HBM procurement for AI accelerators has moved partly toward multi-year agreements rather than commodity spot pricing. Advanced packaging capacity is a harder constraint than general fab capacity, since it is less readily redirected to other product lines.
 Different: the revenue slope. WSTS projects roughly 90% year-over-year industry growth for 2026, against a revised 2025 base of $795.6 billion. Whether that rate persists past 2026 is a separate question, and the forecast was revised upward mid-cycle.
 Familiar: capacity committed near a high. Capital is being committed on the assumption that demand persists. The table above records prices only and carries no capital-spending data, so it cannot confirm that sequence; the dollar detail is in AI semiconductor spending .
 Familiar: valuation above the market. As of July 17, 2026, LSEG weighted forward price-to-earnings ratios reported by MarketWatch stood at 22.8 for SOXX against 20.3 for the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust. A premium is normal for the sector; whether its size is supported by the demand trajectory is the open question.
 §6 — Still Underway The 2026 Decline, Counted Separately
 The SOX set a closing high of 14,634.72 on June 22, 2026 , then fell to a subsequent low of 10,447.49 on July 29 — a decline of −28.61% . It closed at 11,430.35 on August 3, leaving it −21.90% below the June peak at the cutoff.

 Two things happened alongside the decline. SIA’s reported monthly sales continued rising through May 2026. And SOXX took in a record $6.92 billion of net inflows during July, its largest month since inception, according to TradingView fund-flow data cited by Benzinga . Money went in while prices fell.
 Contributing factors cited in contemporaneous reporting included scrutiny of hyperscaler capital-expenditure plans and repricing in memory names. The available evidence does not isolate a single cause.
 §7 — Where the Data Leaves Us A Record, Not a Schedule
 This article previously presented a hand-assembled five-row table and drew a threshold claim from it. Recomputing from the source series replaced that approach entirely: the earlier selection omitted qualifying episodes and misstated at least one figure, and any threshold read off it reflected which episodes had been picked rather than a property of the sector.
 What the computed record supports is narrower. Within the available series, declines of 20% or more from a closing high occurred seven times in under twenty-two years, ranging from −25.05% to −68.66%, with observed recovery intervals from 2.6 to 62.1 months. That describes frequency and dispersion in one dataset. It is not a forecast of depth or timing.
 The 2026 decline sits outside that record because it has not resolved. A −28.61% drawdown alongside record revenue forecasts and record fund inflows does not settle into either the cyclical or the structural story. It is data, not a verdict.
 This semiconductor cycle history is a record of what a defined dataset shows under a stated rule. Whether current conditions differ enough to break the pattern is a question the data frames but does not answer. 
 What The Data Shows
 Within Nasdaq SOX daily closes from September 2, 2004 to August 3, 2026, seven completed episodes met a 20% peak-to-trough threshold, ranging −25.05% to −68.66% . Observed recovery intervals ran 2.6 to 62.1 months. Episode 1 spans a data gap from May 20 to October 31, 2008 and is not verified to the same standard as the others.
 Industry sales have declined in individual years while the long-run trend rose — $139B in 2001, a revised $795.6B for 2025, with WSTS forecasting roughly $1.51T for 2026.
 A decline that began June 22, 2026 remains unresolved at the cutoff: −28.61% to the July 29 subsequent low, −21.90% at the August 3 close. It is excluded from the table because it is incomplete, not because of its size. This article draws no directional conclusion from it.
