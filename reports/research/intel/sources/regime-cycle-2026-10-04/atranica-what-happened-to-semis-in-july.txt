 What Happened to Semiconductors in July?

 Igor Rotor, CFA 
 Aug 07, 2026 

 3

 Share

 The AI spending boom did not collapse. An earnings-backed rally became the market’s most crowded trade, and leverage turned a reassessment into a rout. 
 Semiconductor stocks entered the summer as one of the strongest trades in global markets. They left July looking like one of the most violent unwinds since the financial crisis.
 The PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index (SOX) lost about 21% in July, its worst monthly performance since October 2008. By contrast, the S&P 500 Total Return Index slipped just 0.1%, underscoring how narrowly the market damage was concentrated. Even after an 8.3% rebound over the final two sessions, the SOX finished roughly 23% below its June 22 record. The July selloff erased around $2.2 trillion from the combined market capitalization of SOX constituents.

 Volatility was equally striking: almost half of July’s sessions ended with a move of at least 4% in either direction, while every trading day produced an intraday range of 2% or more.

 Yet the selloff did not coincide with a collapse in near-term AI demand. Hyperscalers continued to commit extraordinary sums to infrastructure, and earnings expectations for leading chipmakers remained robust. That apparent contradiction is the key to understanding July.
 The market was no longer asking whether AI spending was strong. It was asking whether the rate of growth in spending, chip scarcity, pricing power and margins could remain exceptional for long enough to justify prices—and whether investors had become too crowded in the same answer.
 The rally was supported by earnings 

 It would be too simple to call the first-half surge an empty AI bubble. FactSet data compiled by Goldman Sachs show that semiconductor prices broadly followed a powerful rise in consensus forward earnings from the start of 2025. The clearest evidence came from memory, where AI servers created an acute requirement for high-bandwidth memory, tightened the wider DRAM market and drove a sharp improvement in pricing and profits. 

 That fundamental acceleration helped semiconductor shares more than double by the June peak. But the relationship changed near the top: prices began to run ahead of forward earnings estimates. The trade was still supported by fundamentals, but it had become increasingly dependent on further upgrades.
 At the same time, positioning became extraordinary. The Bank of America Fund Manager Survey shows how quickly the trade became crowded. The share of respondents identifying “long global semiconductors” as the world’s most crowded trade rose from 24% in April to 73% in May, 80% in June and 82% in July. That did not tell investors when prices would reverse, but it did describe the market’s vulnerability. When almost everyone shares the same thesis, even a modest reduction in confidence can produce a disproportionate move.

 The break began before July 

 The correction began on June 23. By then, the PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index, or SOX, had more than doubled during the first half of 2026 and reached a record high.

 Several concerns arrived at once. Rising Treasury yields increased the discount rate applied to long-duration growth assets. Investors were also becoming more sensitive to the financing and free-cash-flow consequences of the AI buildout. Most visibly, the pressure spread to Korea: the KOSPI fell almost 10%, triggering a circuit breaker, while Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dropped 12.3% and 12.5%, respectively. 
 At roughly the same time, industry reports said SK Hynix was slowing its HBM4 ramp and allocating more capacity according to relative profitability across HBM and conventional DRAM. That did not mean AI demand had disappeared. If anything, it reflected the strength of broader memory economics. But the market could interpret it as the first crack in a much more powerful assumption: permanently scarce AI chips, uninterrupted capacity growth and ever-rising margins. 
 No single item was large enough to explain the subsequent decline. Together, however, they stopped the one-way momentum that had carried the sector through the first half.
 July changed the question 

 One of the first July catalysts arrived on July 1, when Bloomberg reported that Meta was developing a cloud-infrastructure business that could sell external customers access to AI models and raw computing power. The plans were still in development, but the reference to “ excess computing power ” raised the possibility that hyperscalers could eventually redirect capacity built for internal use into the external market. Meta jumped 9.3% in early trading, while CoreWeave and Nebius fell as much as 14% and 17%, respectively. 
 The report did not show that Meta already had idle capacity or that AI demand was weakening. Bloomberg said demand remained exceptionally strong, and Zuckerberg had previously said Meta still expected to use its compute. For semiconductor investors, the significance lay in future economics rather than current demand: if installed capacity could be monetized externally, utilization and returns on AI investment might become more important than capacity additions alone. That challenged supplier valuations dependent on continued capex acceleration, persistent scarcity and exceptional margins.
 Other concerns reinforced that reassessment during the month: rapid progress by Chinese memory and equipment companies, the efficiency of open-source AI models, the possibility of a more conventional memory cycle, and repeated questions about the returns on hyperscaler capital spending. Strong semiconductor results were sometimes met with weak share-price reactions—a classic sign that high expectations, rather than weak current numbers, had become the problem.
 The data do not support a simple month-long rotation from semiconductors into software. The two groups did not consistently move in opposite directions. A clearer divergence emerged only late in July, when selected hyperscalers were rewarded for evidence of cloud growth, AI monetization and spending discipline. Chip suppliers, by contrast, were priced not merely for high capex, but for continued acceleration in capex, persistent scarcity and exceptional margins.
 AI spending is a level. Equity returns depend on changes in the market’s expectations for that level and for the profits it will generate.
 Crowding turned a reassessment into liquidation 

 Profit-taking describes how the decline spread, but not what initiated it. Once the upward trend broke, the structure of the trade amplified every piece of bad news.
 The rally had attracted momentum strategies, leveraged portfolios and investors using the same group of liquid stocks as a concentrated expression of the AI theme. Falling prices then tightened risk limits, generated margin calls and forced sales. Forced deleveraging at the highly leveraged Situational Awareness fund was a vivid example: its portfolio combined long AI beneficiaries with short positions in software, leaving both sides exposed when the prevailing factor relationships reversed. It was an accelerant, not the original cause of the industry selloff.
 The result was an unusually disorderly sequence. SOXX fell 11.6% over July 1–3, rebounded only 2.7% the following week, then lost another 10.2% over July 13–17. Subsequent rebounds were again shallow, and the ETF finished July down 22.1% despite a rally of roughly 9% over the final two days.
 The damage was broad. Every SOX constituent finished below its June 22 level, and more than half lost at least 25%. Performance from the peak nevertheless varied sharply: Broadcom and Nvidia slipped just 0.7% and 3.8%, respectively, while Micron and Intel fell 32.1% and 36.0%. The market was not abandoning every company exposed to AI equally. It was repricing the most stretched assumptions and the portfolios most vulnerable to forced selling.

 Record inflows into a falling market 

 The most revealing feature of the selloff may be what happened inside semiconductor ETFs.
 Five broad, unlevered US semiconductor ETFs—SOXX, SMH, XSD, SOXQ and PSI—received approximately $11.8 billion in July, following $6.3 billion in June. SOXX and SMH alone attracted about $11.4 billion, a record combined monthly inflow. SOXX received $6.9 billion and SMH $4.5 billion. 

 The concentration was remarkable. About 96% of the unlevered July inflow went into SOXX and SMH, while the equal-weighted XSD received almost nothing. On July 8 alone, SOXX absorbed $5.4 billion—nearly four-fifths of its entire monthly inflow.
 Leveraged products showed even stronger dip-buying. SOXL, which targets three times the daily semiconductor return, attracted another $6.9 billion in July. It should be analyzed separately from long-only funds because its daily reset, derivatives and rebalancing mechanics make its flows economically different. Its large inflow also followed substantial earlier redemptions, so it cannot simply be added to conventional ETF demand and interpreted dollar for dollar.
 How could record inflows coexist with a historic price decline? Because ETF flows measure only one route into the market. Buyers of ETF shares were absorbing stock supplied by sellers elsewhere: active funds, single-stock investors, leveraged portfolios, derivatives dealers and international holders. Authorized participants could create new ETF shares even as the underlying basket fell if selling through other channels remained larger.
 The flow data therefore point to a large transfer of ownership. Momentum and leveraged holders appear to have reduced exposure while a new cohort of dip-buyers accumulated the most liquid ETFs. The inflows may have softened the decline and helped the late-month rebound, but they do not prove that the bottom was reached—or that crowding was fully cleared. Indeed, a market that continues to fall despite record demand from a visible group of buyers is also revealing how much supply remains. 
 What July did—and did not—change 

 July did not establish that the AI investment cycle was over. Hyperscaler capex remained strong, forward earnings were still high, and the final two sessions showed how quickly semiconductor shares could rebound once forced selling eased.
 What changed was the burden of proof. Investors stopped rewarding exposure to AI infrastructure indiscriminately. They began distinguishing between companies demonstrating returns on AI investment and suppliers whose valuations required capacity growth, scarcity and margins to remain exceptional.
 That leaves four signals worth watching after the selloff:
 · Forward earnings revisions. If estimates continue to rise while prices stabilize, the correction will increasingly look like a valuation and positioning reset. Falling estimates would point to a deeper fundamental turn. 
 · Hyperscaler capex and free cash flow. The issue is not merely how much they spend, but whether spending continues to accelerate and produces visible revenue. 
 · Memory pricing, capacity and margins. HBM demand can remain strong while a shift in the wider memory cycle changes the earnings profile of the suppliers. 
 · Price behavior relative to ETF flows. Continued inflows with weak prices would suggest persistent distribution. Stabilizing prices on less extreme flows would be healthier evidence that ownership has reset. 
 The best description of July is therefore not that the AI boom ended. An extraordinary, earnings-backed advance became the market’s most crowded trade. A cluster of concerns then challenged its most optimistic assumptions, and leverage converted that reassessment into a historic liquidation.
 The fundamentals created the rally. Positioning determined the violence of its reversal.
