## Findings

### Q1: Academic industry/sector predictability with ML or shrinkage

- **Rapach, Strauss, Tu, Zhou (2019, JFDS)** — LASSO on lagged industry returns, controlling explicitly for post-selection inference and multiple testing (a real methodological safeguard against data-mined predictors). Consistent search-snippet claim across 4 passes: lagged returns of financial and commodity/materials industries have the widest predictive power; an OOS industry-rotation long/short portfolio (long highest-forecast, short lowest-forecast) delivers **>8%/yr annualized alpha** vs standard multi-factor models. **Not independently confirmed**: exact sample end date, number of industries (30 vs 49 Fama-French), net-of-transaction-cost figure, or explicit comparison to plain lagged-own-industry momentum — all blocked from direct reading. Given the Oct-2018 SSRN posting date, the sample almost certainly ends by **~2016-2017**, i.e. **before** the "especially after 2018" window this task asks about — this paper cannot itself answer that question.
- **Hong, Torous, Valkanov (2007, JFE)** — a different claim than "which sector leads other sectors": specific US industries (retail, services, commercial real estate, metal, petroleum) lead the **aggregate stock market** by up to two months via gradual information diffusion, and an industry's propensity to lead correlates with its propensity to forecast real economic activity. A later "Note" on this paper exists (found, not readable) — its purpose (correction vs. extension) could not be confirmed.
- **Menzly & Ozbas (2010, JF)** — supply-chain-linked (customer/supplier) industries cross-predict each other's returns; the effect's magnitude **declines with analyst coverage and institutional ownership** — i.e. the paper's own mechanism predicts erosion as more capital watches it, which is a built-in reason to expect decay in a well-covered, ETF-saturated market like today's.
- **Moskowitz & Grinblatt (1999, JF)** — industry momentum explains most of individual-stock momentum; **has third-party replication**: a 2009 student replication (Fraulo & Nguyen, US large caps 1998-2007) confirmed the core effect and found industry momentum more profitable than stock-level momentum in that later window. This is the one link in the whole chain with an actual (if modest — student-paper-grade) independent replication.
- **Recent (2020s) ML/regime sector-rotation papers found, all preprints or lower-tier venues, none independently verified**: RegimeFolio (internally inconsistent numbers, see table), Online Ensemble Learning for Sector Rotation (no numbers extractable), Two-Stage Sector Rotation (unreadable), and a TSX-60 (Canadian, not US) MDPI/JRFM paper claiming Sharpe 0.922 vs 0.624 buy-and-hold with 2020-2025 OOS validation and 72.7% regime-classification accuracy across 72 tested variants — found only via search snippet, not opened, and MDPI/JRFM is a lower-rigor venue than the finance-field top journals above.
- **None of the above, where a comparison to plain 3-12 month industry momentum was findable, showed a clear, cost-inclusive beat of it.** Quantpedia's own recent (through Sep 2024) enhanced-momentum backtest tops out at Sharpe ~0.6-0.7 — a real number, but it is momentum-plus-regime-gate, not a distinct forecasting mechanism, and not spectacular.

### Q2: Post-publication and third-party replication

- **No open-source GitHub replication of the Rapach et al. lead-lag/LASSO result was found.** GitHub search surfaced only unrelated small/personal/student sector-rotation repos (momentum + ad hoc ML, no stars/track record signal, one explicit BSc dissertation project) — none replicate the specific published method or report a comparable OOS number.
- **Quantpedia** hosts the Faber sector-momentum strategy as a citation of the original 1928-2009 paper, not as its own re-verified live number; no 2019-2026 discussion present. Its own original research (the momentum+regime-short enhancement) is the most credible recent same-market number found, at Sharpe ~0.6-0.7.
- **Allocate Smartly** directly tested a Fama-French-factor-alpha sector-rotation strategy (their own words, from search synthesis of their published post): performance "marginally better than the benchmark, but not enough to justify trading it," and explicitly states "factor alphas ... do not appear to have significant predictive ability for US stock market sectors." This is a **named, third-party, practitioner-run negative result** for a non-momentum sector-forecast approach, on the closest analog to what this task is asking about.
- **Alpha Architect**: general commentary only — sector-based momentum "could be a reasonable alternative" to broad momentum, with an explicit caveat that off-the-shelf sector classification schemes oversimplify true industry relationships. No specific non-momentum forecast model or live number surfaced.
- No Two Sigma, AQR, or Man Group note specifically validating a **sector**-forecast model was found; Two Sigma's regime-modeling piece is multi-asset/factor-level and explicitly disclaims predictive use (see table).

### Q3: Regime-to-sector mapping, including negative evidence

- **Strongest single finding in this brief**, directly confirmed by opening the source: **Molchanov & Stangl, "The Myth of Business Cycle Sector Rotation" (International Journal of Finance & Economics, 2023, open access)** — no evidence of systematic sector performance where conventional business-cycle wisdom expects it; a relaxed version letting any industry's return predict any other's is statistically indistinguishable from random. Additional numbers from search-engine synthesis only (Wiley full text blocked both attempts, treat as unconfirmed): with perfect foresight and zero costs, best-case outperformance ~2.3%/yr over 1948-2007; of 2,046 tested rotation-strategy variants, only 8.3% beat a market-timing baseline and only 25% beat buy-and-hold.
- **Joubert, Sestovic, Barziy, Distaso, López de Prado, "The Three Types of Backtests" (SSRN 2024 / ADIA Lab)** — methodological, not empirical, but directly on point (search-snippet synthesis, primary PDF unreadable): a regime-based backtest is "unusually easy to fool yourself with" because the number of states, detection method, feature set, and switching penalty are all free choices, each a chance to fit noise; the paper deliberately reports no performance figures and instead argues a detected regime must beat a **randomly-assigned regime** on downside measures before being trusted. This is a directly actionable design constraint (see Q5).
- **Practitioner-level (third-hand, Substack synthesis, weakest tier of evidence in this brief)**: some 2024-2025 factor-timing-with-regimes papers show a real but narrow edge concentrated in **drawdown protection, not return enhancement** (e.g., Sortino/downside-period improvements with weak or no Sharpe gain; one cited paper's OOS 2020-2024 holdout found the regime signal "added essentially nothing"; turnover costs cut gross Sharpe roughly in third in another). This literature is about factor/multi-asset timing, not sector rotation specifically, and none of these individual papers were opened directly.
- **No source found makes a credible, cost-inclusive, post-2018 case that macro regime state (yield curve, inflation, credit spreads, ISM) predicts next-month sector outperformance.** The one rigorous, peer-reviewed, directly-read source on this exact question is negative.

### Q4: Leveraged sector ETF selection specifically

- **arXiv 2504.20116** (opened directly, HTML) is the closest paper to this question and **does not answer it**: it is a single-asset (SPY/QQQ) mechanics paper about how return autocorrelation and volatility clustering determine LETF compounding versus naive volatility-drag intuition. It explicitly does not compare sectors, does not propose a selection rule, assumes frictionless markets for its core proofs (fees/tracking error separately estimated at ~0.8-1.0pp/yr drag), and gives no ex-ante method to detect trending-vs-mean-reverting regimes in real time — the one gap that would matter most for a sector-forecast application.
- No other public work was found on selecting which leveraged sector ETF to hold via a non-momentum mechanism. Retail-blog "sector rotation models" for leveraged ETFs found in search (iQUANT.pro, LeveragedEdge, etc.) are RSI/moving-average trend filters — i.e., decorated momentum, not a distinct forecasting mechanism, and none carry third-party verification or a disclosed live track record.

### Q5: What a cheapest decisive local test would look like

Given Q1-Q4, the honest baseline is: **no source found clears "beats plain 3-12 month industry momentum, net of realistic costs, out-of-sample, after 2018."** If this family is worth even a bounded local test (not a full build), it should be structured as a cheap kill-test, not a strategy build:

- **Baseline first**: reconfirm the existing 63-day plain-momentum ranking already used in the frozen S3/`H-20260923-10` machine as the bar to beat — the test is "beats our own momentum net of our own costs," not "beats buy-and-hold."
- **Tiny, preregistered predictor set** (per the research-mission's bounded-search-space rule), not a fishing expedition: (a) lagged 1-3mo cross-industry returns (Rapach/Hong-Torous-style LASSO on our own SIP daily industry/sector data, 2016-2026), (b) a short macro list — 10y-3m yield curve, ISM manufacturing, a credit-spread proxy — mapped to next-month sector performance (directly re-testing Molchanov's rejected mechanism on our own leveraged-ETF menu), and nothing else.
- **Mandatory placebo before trusting any result**, per Joubert et al. and consistent with the same protocol that already refuted the miners/banks/aero lanes: shuffle/randomize the predicted-winner label across time and require the real signal to clear the placebo distribution, not just beat zero.
- **Cheapest kill condition**: run the LASSO/macro forecast expanding-window OOS **only for 2018-2026** on data already on hand (no new data acquisition) — this is roughly a one-day task, since LASSO is cheap and the data already exists. If OOS R² ≤ 0 in that window, or the signal doesn't clear the placebo band, stop — this matches the reopen condition's actual bar and avoids building anything.
- **Cadence discipline**: test at the same monthly rebalance cadence S3 already uses before considering anything higher-frequency — Quantpedia's own recent, same-market, cost-inclusive number for a related momentum+regime enhancement tops out around Sharpe 0.6-0.7, which is a reasonable ceiling expectation, not a floor.
- If the kill-test above fails (likely, per the weight of evidence in Q1-Q3), **do not** proceed to sourcing new data (options-implied or news-driven industry signals, per the reopen condition's alternative) without a separate, specific reason to believe that data source specifically would clear the same placebo bar.

## Evidence strength verdict: **weak**

There is real, peer-reviewed, foundational literature here (Rapach, Hong-Torous-Valkanov, Menzly-Ozbas, Moskowitz-Grinblatt all exist, are real, and industry momentum itself has genuine third-party replication) — so this is not "none." But every specific claim that a **non-momentum** forecasting mechanism (ML lead-lag or macro-regime) beats plain momentum, net of costs, in a recent sample, failed to clear this pass's bar: the single ML paper making the strongest claim (Rapach et al., >8%/yr alpha) could not be read directly after seven attempts and almost certainly predates 2018 entirely; the 2020s arXiv sector-rotation-ML papers are unreviewed preprints with unreadable, unconfirmed, or internally-inconsistent numbers (RegimeFolio's headline figures don't arithmetically fit its own stated 12-month test window); and the one rigorous, peer-reviewed, directly-opened source that squarely targets "does macro/regime state predict which sector outperforms" (Molchanov & Stangl, 2023) answers **no**, with a companion methodological paper (Joubert et al./López de Prado, 2024) explaining exactly why regime-based backtests are prone to false positives. Nothing at all was found on selecting among leveraged sector ETFs by a non-momentum mechanism; the one closely-related paper (arXiv 2504.20116) is explicitly single-asset mechanics, not a selection model. The reopen condition set by `H-20260923-10` ("a mechanism that forecasts which sector trends next... or new data") is not satisfied by anything found in this pass.
