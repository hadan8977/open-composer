Title: Night trading: Lower risk but higher returns?

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Most investors are unaware that overnight returns exceed intraday returns in the United States, although several works (Berkman et al.,[2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0004); Cliff et al.,[2008](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0005); Kelly & Clark,[2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0013); Lachance,[2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0014)) document this phenomenon. This observation is surprising given that it is well known that volatility is lower during the nighttime period. This paper contributes to the literature in three ways by providing new evidence that the overnight return anomaly is persistent, predictable, and can be profitable after costs. This anomaly deserves more attention as it is not limited to a period, and its superior performance persists several years after portfolio formation.

More specifically, the paper examines two research questions related to overnight returns. First, is there long-term persistence in overnight returns? Regression analysis shows that the past year's overnight return is the most important predictor of future overnight returns, followed by volatility, momentum, and turnover.[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0011_7 "Link to note") Second, can these patterns be exploited profitably and reliably with overnight investment strategies, even after considering risk and costs? The cost of overnight strategies is generally not quantified in current works, which limits their explicit recommendations to delaying purchases to the close and sales to the open. Overnight approaches would be cost-prohibitive with round-trip transaction costs such as those reported in Barber and Odean([2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0002)): 1% for the bid-ask spread and 1.4% for commissions. However, transaction costs have declined sharply over the last decade, and the paper calibrates them with more recent figures. From a cost standpoint, overnight strategies offer an interesting case because they can be implemented with the exchanges' single-price opening and closing call auctions, saving on the bid-ask spread.

Overnight investment strategies should identify stocks with abnormal returns likely to exceed transaction costs. The finding that stocks exhibit persistence in their overnight returns facilitates this identification. This persistence is demonstrated formally with Fama and MacBeth([1973](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0007)) cross-sectional regressions and data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) covering 1995–2021. The regressions show that overnight monthly returns are strongly positively related to each of their lagged values, with lags ranging from one to 60 months. By contrast, the relationship between overnight returns and current and past monthly intraday returns is negative.

These results are not only highly statistically significant but also economically meaningful. To make a parallel with Jegadeesh and Titman's([1993](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0011)) momentum strategy, an “overnight momentum” strategy of picking stocks in the top decile of overnight returns for the past year and holding them overnight for the next year yields an annual return of 37.50%. By contrast, stocks in the bottom decile of past overnight returns underperform overnight with a −18.99% return. Unlike traditional momentum portfolios, overnight profits do not reverse in the next 5 years after forming the decile portfolios.

The means of the overnight and intraday return process are strongly negatively related, which suggests that offsetting biases distort them. This idea is formalized by introducing a new model incorporating an Overnight Bias Parameter (OBP) in the mean of the overnight return process. This parameter is subtracted from the mean of the intraday return process, leaving total returns unaffected. The OBP can be measured empirically as the intercept in a regression of overnight returns on full-day returns. The model's ability to predict the out-of-sample partition of returns is demonstrated by graphing the average overnight and intraday returns for each percentile of the total return distribution, both for predicted and actual values. Despite the model's simplicity, the predicted values fit actual ones exceptionally well, with _R_ 2 s above 0.98. In particular, incorporating biases makes it possible to reconcile the striking empirical observation that average overnight returns are always positive in the period considered, even in the worst percentiles of the total return distribution.

Stocks with OBPs that are positive and significant at the 5% level in the previous year are assigned to the Overnight Bias Group (OBG) and represent a meaningful portion of the CRSP sample, averaging 561 stocks a year or 22% of the sample. In the year following this classification, investing overnight in OBG stocks yields an annual return of 24.68% and a risk-adjusted return of 18.58%. This strategy has a relatively low risk, with a market beta of 0.35 and a standard deviation of 12.09%. The results are robust when considering several alternative methodologies and sub-samples. Most impressively, the risk-adjusted returns for the OBG overnight portfolio are positive in all but one of the 27 years of the sample. The results also have implications for daytime investors, as OBG stocks had negative intraday returns for 23 of the 27 years.

The OBG overnight strategy is viable on an after-cost[2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0012_11 "Link to note") basis with an alpha of 10.36%. By contrast, investing overnight in stocks outside the OBG category is generally a losing proposition when considering costs. The main limitation of the overnight strategy is that there is an implementation risk if participation in the call auctions is insufficient or if large trades have a price impact. While the paper uses the broadest set of stocks possible for statistical purposes, in practice, liquidity and transaction costs should be considered when selecting stocks to include in an overnight strategy. Excluding stocks that are illiquid or that have low share prices does not affect the strategy's performance.

The paper concludes by examining intraday return patterns with a subsample of Trades and Quotes (TAQ) data for 2012–2021. Figure[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0001) previews these results by comparing OBG stocks to other groups of stocks classified by their OBP. This illustration includes after-hours trading and shows that the abnormal overnight return process for OBG stocks starts after the close, continues the following morning, and peaks minutes before the market officially opens at 9:30 a.m. Abnormal overnight returns decrease after the open but do not disappear immediately. One can avoid intraday losses for OBG stocks by waiting 30–45 min after the open to purchase them. Stocks with negative OBPs display opposite patterns. By mid-day, all groups of stocks behave similarly.

## 3 PERSISTENCE OF OVERNIGHT RETURNS

This paper's thesis is that some stocks tend to perform better overnight and that this trait persists from one period to the next. A sufficient number of stocks with positive overnight biases can explain the presence of excess overnight returns at the market level.

Table[2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0002 "Link to table") shows that the decile portfolios do not have significant differences in total returns; however, there is a clear relationship between the deciles and the partition of overnight and intraday returns. For the top-decile portfolio, overnight returns are 37.50%, and intraday returns are −18.99%. Results for the bottom-decile portfolio are the opposite: a −11.68% return overnight and a 24.92% return intraday. The risk-adjusted results indicate similar trends. Portfolios in the middle deciles display a smooth progression between these two extremes. These figures are more substantial than the 0.68% monthly return that Jegadeesh and Titman([1993](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0011)) report for the difference between the top and bottom-decile momentum portfolios. While the traditional concept of momentum is often associated with waves of optimism or pessimism, the last column of Table[2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0002 "Link to table") shows that a continuation in superior total returns does not accompany the continuation of superior overnight returns and that the phenomena are different.

There is a possibility that the top-decile portfolio picks up stocks with high overnight return means. Jegadeesh and Titman([2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0012)) suggest that the presence of mean effects can be tested by evaluating the performance of the decile portfolios several years after the portfolios' formation. If momentum performance is due to higher means, it should persist over time. They find momentum profits are positive for the first 12 months following the formation period but then turn negative after 13 to 60 months. The bottom of Table[2](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0002 "Link to table") repeats this exercise with overnight returns by evaluating the performance of the decile portfolios in each of the 5 years following the portfolios' formation. Overnight returns for the top-decile portfolio are 22.87% (after 1 year), 20.43% (after 2 years), 20.85% (after 3 years), 17.73% (after 4 years), and 14.17% (after 5 years). The results provide evidence that long-term means play a role in our results: performance attenuates over time but does not reverse or disappear.

If the profitability of an overnight strategy is based on picking stocks with higher expected overnight returns, then the profits of an overnight momentum strategy might simply be a normal reward for selecting stocks with characteristics priced by the market. For instance, stocks in the top decile are riskier, and observing higher returns can be consistent with higher risk. However, this argument does not explain why these riskier stocks simultaneously have the lowest intraday returns and, in particular, why these returns are negative. While recognizing that characteristics priced by the market can explain part of the performance of the overnight momentum strategy, this paper argues that there is more behind the cross-sectional variations in overnight return means than traditional relationships with characteristics. Specifically, the means of the overnight and intraday return processes appear distorted by offsetting biases. The fact that there is more dispersion in the means of overnight and intraday returns than in the means of total daily returns suggests the presence of reversing biases. This idea is also consistent with the earlier finding that the means of overnight and intraday returns have a negative relationship in the cross-section. It would explain why they have opposite associations with characteristics such as risk. Section[4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-sec-0007) will incorporate these biases in an overnight and intraday returns model.

## 4 OVERNIGHT BIAS MODEL

This section introduces an Overnight Bias Model with offsetting biases in the means of the overnight and intraday return processes. The literature is unclear regarding how to model returns during nontrading (overnight) periods. For instance, in his work on the weekend effect, French([1980](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0008)) contrasts a calendar time hypothesis by which returns accrue continuously and a trading time hypothesis by which returns are generated only during active trading; he finds that returns are inconsistent with both models. One point generally agreed upon is that there is less activity overnight than intraday: French and Roll([1986](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0009)) find that more private information is incorporated during the day; George and Hwang([2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0010)) study the rate of information flow during trading and nontrading periods and estimate that the median daytime rate is about seven times higher than the overnight rate; Barclay and Hendershott([2003](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0003)) examine after-hours trading and attribute 85% of the weighted price contribution to the daytime period.

### 4.1 Model

Using log returns, a stock's total return can be written as _r=r O+r I_. In the absence of abnormal returns, the model assumes that, on average, a component _θr_ of returns is earned overnight, and the balance _(1-θ)r_ accrues intraday. Without any bias in the distribution of positive/negative news, the parameter θ should capture differences in information flow and be higher for firms with more overnight activity. Following George and Hwang's([2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0010)) finding that more information is incorporated in prices during the daytime, the model assumes that _θ_<1/2. Up to this point, the model predicts that daytime returns should be higher than overnight returns in absolute terms. Overnight returns in excess of _θr_ represent an “overnight bias,” denoted by a parameter OBP. Each stock can have a different OBP. Since the overnight bias does not affect the stock's total return prospects, intraday returns must be adjusted by a corresponding negative mirror effect −OBP. This fit is an improvement over an alternative in which overnight returns would be projected simply as a fraction of total returns, which would yield an _R_ 2 of 0.85. Of course, the fit here concerns average values; individual overnight returns in any given year can be noisy, and the model only attempts to predict their expected values.

Besides showing that the model fits the data well, Figure[3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0003) provides a surprising new empirical result: average overnight returns are positive for each percentile, even for the bottom ones. Again, it should be kept in mind that these are long-term averages and that overnight returns can be negative in any particular year. The model predicts and reconciles the puzzling empirical observation in Figure[3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0003). Two factors explain why overnight returns are so resilient when stocks perform poorly. The first one is not related to biases but, instead, to the _θr_ portion of the model. Because overnight returns are less sensitive to total returns, they will be less affected than intraday returns in a downturn. Based on the average estimate for _θ_ of 19%, a loss of −10% in total returns translates into a loss of only −1.9% in overnight returns. The second factor is the “bonus” overnight return provided by the parameter OBP, which helps offset losses. This bonus is more significant for stocks that perform more poorly. For instance, the average OBP is 20.35% for stocks in the bottom decile of total returns, which is sufficient to hedge most losses and even move returns into positive territory.[7](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0017_62 "Link to note")

Finally, Figure[3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0003) provides some insights into overnight spreads—that is, the difference between overnight and intraday returns. The literature has primarily equated positive overnight spreads with abnormal overnight returns. However, Figure[3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0003) and the Overnight Bias Model show that this measure has limitations, as it is systematically related to performance: overnight spreads are positive in poor performance scenarios and negative in good ones. Thus, observing positive overnight spreads in market downturns does not imply that overnight returns are positively biased; this result should be expected. Similarly, negative overnight spreads when the market outperforms do not contradict the existence of overnight biases. More precisely, the model predicts that overnight returns dominate full-day returns to the extent that the latter do not exceed a threshold OBP/(1-_θ_).

### 5.1 Transaction costs

Table[4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0004 "Link to table") also illustrates the potential impact of commissions and fees. Historically, high-turnover strategies were often dismissed due to high transaction costs. This is changing as most retail brokers have recently adopted zero-commission models. However, cost structures without commissions do not mean zero cost, as other fees may apply. Commissions do not usually cover regulatory expenses. For example, the fee under Section 31 of the Securities and Exchange Act is 0.00229% of sales as of February 4, 2023, and there is a Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) charge of $0.000145 per share as of January 1, 2023.

Fees can vary among brokers, and this section illustrates costs with the pricing structure of Interactive Brokers' Lite accounts.[10](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0020_83 "Link to note") These accounts do not charge commissions, but there is a fee of $0.005 per share when orders in the auctions or after-hours exceed 10% of a client's trading activity. As a worst-case scenario, the illustrations assume that the $0.005 charge per share applies twice daily and that the SEC and FINRA fees are paid.[11](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0021_84 "Link to note") These costs are value-weighted and subtracted daily from the portfolio's performance—their daily average is 2.6 bps. For OBG stocks, the resulting after-cost alpha is 10.36%, confirming that the overnight strategy can be profitable after considering realistic transaction costs. The other columns in Table[4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0004 "Link to table") show that overnight strategies are worthwhile primarily for OBG stocks once costs are recognized. Stocks with positive but insignificant OBPs do not have a positive after-cost alpha, nor does the overall market. The impact of costs is more noticeable for the long-short strategy because they apply to each leg of the trade. Still, this strategy is very lucrative, with an after-cost alpha of 17.35%.

Of course, this is a forward-looking exercise, and these results do not imply that the strategy would have been successful 27 years ago with the commission rates that applied at that time. While the illustrations include all securities, ceteris paribus, overnight strategies should use stocks with the lowest transaction costs in practice. For instance, per-share costs are cheaper for higher-priced stocks.

## 6 IMPLEMENTATION WITH THE OPENING AND CLOSING CALL AUCTIONS

An analysis of transaction costs would often also include an allowance for bid-ask spreads. The overnight strategy presents a singular opportunity to avoid these costs by participating in the exchanges' single-price call auctions at the open and close. NASDAQ started the Opening and Closing Crosses in 2004 to facilitate price discovery. Traders can enter Market-on-Open (MOO) orders until 9:28 a.m. and Limit-on-Open (LOO) orders until 9:29:30 a.m. These orders execute at the opening price at 9:30 a.m. Similarly, traders can enter Market-on-Close (MOC) orders until 3:55 p.m. and Limit-on-Close (LOC) orders until 3:58 p.m. They execute at the closing price at 4:00 p.m. An algorithm determines the clearing prices in the crosses to maximize the number of contracts executed. The NYSE offers similar MOC/LOC orders that must be entered by 3:50 p.m. and MOO/LOO orders that can be entered until the opening. The NYSE differs from NASDAQ because the Designated Market Maker can delay the opening.[12](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0022_93 "Link to note")

The auction facilities make it straightforward to implement an overnight investment strategy by entering MOO and MOC orders ahead of time. The 2012–2021 subsample of TAQ data provides information about the call auctions. The data indicate that NASDAQ stocks, on average, open and close promptly at 9:30:00 a.m. and 4:00:00 p.m. For NYSE auctions, the averages are slightly later at 9:30:20 a.m. and 4:02:07 p.m. The TAQ call auction prices are often the same as the opening and closing prices reported in CRSP.[13](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0023_94 "Link to note") However, discrepancies can arise because of market fragmentation, which typically happens with NYSE-listed stocks since they can trade on other exchanges in the few seconds it takes the NYSE to open them. CRSP prices match TAQ's opening and closing auction prices 88% and 99% of the time, respectively.

The TAQ data are used to test the sensitivity of returns to timing trades precisely at the open and the close. Minute-by-minute returns are constructed by dividing the last trade price for that minute by the previous day's closing price.[14](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0024_95 "Link to note") The calculations include after-hours trading, which starts at 4:00 a.m. and ends at 8:00 p.m. If a stock does not trade in a given minute, the last trade price is used. Note that stocks trade more infrequently before the market opens, and returns can be stale. Prices are adjusted for dividends and splits.[15](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0025_96 "Link to note") Figure[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0001) presented earlier gives the results separately for the four OBP classifications used in Tables[4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0004 "Link to table") and [5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-tbl-0005 "Link to table"). Specifically, the portfolios are sorted by the previous year's OBPs, the classification is rebalanced monthly, and returns are market-weighted using the previous day's market capitalization. The period counts, on average, 2495 stocks, 608 of which are in the OBG category.

Figure[1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-fig-0001) reveals an unmistakable gap between OBG and IBG stocks before the opening. The 9:30 a.m. daily average return for OBG stocks is 0.077% versus 0.001% for IBG stocks; annualized, this represents a 21.2% difference.[16](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-note-0026_101 "Link to note") There is much more trading activity for OBG stocks early in the morning, with twice the trading volume in the opening auctions as the IBG stocks. Barclay and Hendershott([2003](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0003)) study extended-hours trading and describe this environment as dominated by professional and quasi-professional traders. This observation suggests that more-sophisticated traders can be behind abnormal overnight returns, although it does not rule out that other investors play an indirect role through their anticipated demand.

Excess overnight returns for OBG stocks start after the close, continue the following morning, and peak a few minutes before the market officially opens at 9:30 a.m. They disappear quickly after the open, but not instantaneously. The gaps between the four groups are much smaller after 10:00 a.m. and disappear in the afternoon. For daytime investors, the flip side of excess overnight returns is that OBG stocks tend to experience intraday losses in the first minutes of trading. The graph indicates that, on average, these losses can be avoided by waiting 30–45 min after the opening to purchase these stocks.

One of the limitations of the strategy suggested in this section is that it might be difficult to implement large trades in stocks with limited liquidity at the open. While price impact is a concern for larger trades, the auction mechanisms attempt to mitigate this issue by periodically broadcasting order imbalances to attract liquidity to the auctions. The average daily volumes of OBG stocks' opening and closing auction trades are $1.1 million and $6.7 million, representing 9.2% of the day's trading volume.

Whether liquidity is an issue for an overnight strategy depends on the type of stock involved. Mega-cap stocks, such as Apple, should have no problem accommodating trades of a reasonable size with over $100 million per auction. At the other extreme, some stocks have limited volume and are less desirable candidates for an overnight strategy. For example, 14% (31%) of OBG stocks in terms of market capitalization have a cumulative volume of less than $1 million ($5 million) in the first 15 min of trading. These stocks are identifiable in advance, and excluding them does not reduce overnight returns for OBG stocks. Between the two extreme cases lies a scenario in which trades get only partially executed in the opening auction and have to be completed in the subsequent continuous auction. Investors should be mindful that executing trades farther from the open can reduce returns.

## 7 CONCLUSION

The paper's title asks, “Night trading: Lower risk but higher returns?” The answer is yes, but only for stocks with positive overnight biases in the past. When accounting for transaction costs, the affirmative answer is limited to stocks with _significant_ and positive overnight biases in the past. The paper makes several contributions to the overnight return literature: it demonstrates that overnight biases are persistent over time, can be modeled, and can be used as the basis for developing profitable investment strategies even after deducting transaction costs. These results should interest investors concerned with the optimal timing of trades, portfolio managers looking for alpha-generating strategies, market makers holding overnight inventories, and researchers working with opening prices.

To conclude, an open question for future research is: What causes overnight biases? The existing literature points to different directions in that regard: Kelly and Clark([2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0013)) attribute the effect to semiprofessional traders who sell their positions at the end of the day and reestablish them in the morning; Berkman et al.([2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0004)) emphasize the role of retail investors and hypothesize that their herding behavior in high-attention stocks pushes opening prices up; Lou et al.([2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0015)) highlight the negative intraday pressure that institutional investors put on momentum stocks; Lachance([2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rfe.1180#rfe1180-bib-0014)) points out the impact of order imbalances interacting with market microstructure effects. Whether or not investors attempt to exploit the overnight bias directly, they should, at the very least, be aware of statistical headwinds that may affect their strategies.
