 
 Prediction Markets: Good Forecasts, Imperfect Prices 
 Antonio Marrazzo Subscribe Sign in Prediction Markets: Good Forecasts, Imperfect Prices What recent research says about event markets as information sources, and where the arbitrage actually was Antonio Marrazzo Sep 14, 2026 2 Share Introduction Prediction markets have moved from academic curiosity to financial infrastructure. Polymarket settles billions of dollars of volume on-chain, Kalshi lists regulated event contracts on everything from inflation prints to Fed decisions, and traders increasingly quote event probabilities the way they quote implied volatility. The research has moved with them, and it asks two distinct questions. First, are the prices good forecasts, good enough to use as inputs to other models? Second, are the prices efficient, or is there money in the cracks between and within these markets? This article reviews the recent research in our library on both questions, spanning the Intrade era of 2012 through the on-chain Polymarket record of 2024 and 2025. It is a review of what other studies report, not a fresh backtest, and every figure below is quoted directly from the paper that produced it. Thanks for reading! As forecasters, prediction markets are genuinely good, often better than the professional alternative. As markets, they are young, fragmented, and structurally imperfect, which for more than a decade has left real, measurable arbitrage on the table. The catch is that the cracks are small in dollar terms, and the size of the documented profits says as much about the limits of these markets as about the opportunity. Background: what an event price is A prediction-market contract pays one dollar if an event happens and zero if it does not, so its price is, to a first approximation, the market’s probability of the event. That simple structure creates two accounting identities that matter for everything below. Within a market whose outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, the prices of all outcomes should sum to one dollar; if you can buy the full set for less, or sell it for more, the difference is riskless profit at resolution. And across venues listing the same event, the two prices should agree; if they do not, buying on one and selling on the other locks in the gap. Neither identity enforces itself. Order books are thin, participants are siloed by geography and regulation, and until recently no arbitrage capital specialized in the space. The research below is largely the story of those identities failing in public. Prices as forecasts: better than the analysts The cleanest evidence that event prices carry real information comes from corporate earnings. Gomez-Cram, Guo, Jensen, and Kung (2025), in “Financial Prediction Markets,” study contracts on whether companies beat earnings expectations and find the market-implied probability correctly classifies 68 percent of announcements seven days ahead and 77 percent one day ahead, against 62 percent for analyst forecasts issued in the same window. The crowd, trading real money, beats the professionals whose job is the forecast, and it improves as information accumulates toward the event. The same study documents a bias worth remembering. Markets priced 81 percent of contracts above fifty cents the day before the announcement, implying beats, while the realized beat rate was 74 percent, and the average price one day out was 0.71. So the prices rank outcomes well but lean optimistic in level. That distinction, good discrimination with imperfect calibration, is exactly what a practitioner needs to know before using raw prices as probabilities: they need a calibration adjustment, not a rebuild. A 2026 study of Kalshi macro contracts asks whether event prices help forecast cryptocurrency volatility and finds they do. Starting from a standard volatility model with an adjusted R-squared of 0.093 for Bitcoin, adding conventional controls lifts it to 0.141, and adding the prediction-market signals lifts it again to 0.155. The individual signals are strongly significant: a Fed-dovish contract carries a t-statistic of positive 3.63 for Bitcoin volatility, a recession-risk contract negative 2.76, and inflation contracts move the volatility of Ethereum, Solana, Cardano, and Chainlink with t-statistics between negative 2.1 and negative 3.4. The signs differ by contract and asset, which is itself informative: dovish surprises raise crypto volatility while inflation-print certainty lowers it in the majors. Event prices, in other words, are now a usable input for risk models in an entirely different asset class. Figure 2. Bars show Newey-West t-statistics. Reported figures from the study. Even the classic manipulation worry looks manageable in the base case. A 2026 agent-based study builds a simulated election market with one hundred trading agents, each with its own budget, expertise, stubbornness, and risk aversion, and measures how far the market price strays from the true evolving outcome. The market’s mean squared error holds near its baseline of about 0.05 across most of the parameter space: accuracy survives wide variation in expertise, budgets, and risk aversion, and degrades materially only when average stubbornness rises above 0.8, meaning traders largely stop updating on new information, or when biases are pushed systematically to one side. The same model shows analytically that a whale moves the price in proportion to its share of market capital, which is the honest version of the manipulation concern: it is a capital-share problem in thin markets, not a standing indictment of event prices. The cracks, act one: the 2012 record The efficiency story has a clear arc, and it begins with Rothschild and Pennock (2014), who documented cross-venue misalignment in the Intrade-Betfair era with a thoroughness that still repays reading. On the 2012 United States election, the Betfair bid for the Obama contract exceeded the Intrade ask on every day of the year except two, the two prices sat more than five percentage points apart for 64 of the final 85 days, and the misalignment peaked at over 23 percentage points at five minutes past eight on election night. These were identical contracts on the same event, and they disagreed for months. What makes the paper definitive is that the authors did not stop at observation. They ran a live, randomized field trial, investing $3,686 of real money in the arbitrage and earning 6.38 percent over three months, net of all transaction costs; their largest single investment of $1,841.80 returned $135.51 after costs. They also showed the visible order book understates the opportunity. Reconstructing the shadow order book, the depth that would have appeared as visible orders were lifted, they estimate a conservative net take of $15,000 to $20,000 over the closing months of the election, against $1,000 to $5,000 available from closing only the visible book: an order of magnitude more. Even the manipulation angle is quantified: over the final two weeks, a single trader accounted for 35 percent of all money supporting Romney on Intrade, paying prices the authors estimate carried a 20 to 25 percent premium to Betfair. Bars show the reported ranges for the net take over the closing months of the 2012 election. Reported figures from the study. The same paper documents the within-market identity failing too. In the New Hampshire primary market for second place, a trader could momentarily sell the full candidate bundle for $1.082, or buy it for $0.591, against a certain value of one dollar. And it documents a systematic asymmetry with a behavioral fingerprint: sampling the bundle daily, the sum of asks strayed from one dollar more often than the sum of bids, consistent with longshot sellers demanding too much for unlikely outcomes. The cracks, act two: the on-chain record A decade later the venues changed and the pattern did not, but now the ledger is public. Saguillo and coauthors (2025), in “Unravelling the Probabilistic Forest,” measure arbitrage on Polymarket over the year to April 2025 directly from on-chain data: 17,218 outcome conditions across 8,659 single-condition and 1,578 multi-condition markets, reconstructed from 86.6 million executed bids. They formalize two arbitrage types, rebalancing within a single market whose outcome prices fail to sum to one, and combinatorial arbitrage across logically dependent markets, using a language model to detect which markets are logically linked. The totals are striking: 7,051 conditions, about 41 percent, exhibited at least one detectable opportunity, 662 of the 1,578 multi-condition markets did, and the realized profit actually extracted by traders totals $39,587,585 over the year, with a median profit of about $0.60 per dollar on detected single-condition opportunities. The single most striking trade in their data bought both the yes and the no of one market for under two cents each, locking in $58,983 at resolution. The freshest work brings the story to the 2024 election and adds the microstructure. A 2025 study of price discovery across Polymarket, Kalshi, and PredictIt, on samples with total volumes of $1.1 billion and $580 million respectively, finds persistent level disparities: the Harris contract priced higher on Kalshi than Polymarket on all 33 overlapping days, higher on PredictIt on 304 of 306 days, with average gaps of about 3.27 cents on Harris and 2.59 cents on Trump. Polymarket leads price discovery: a 1 percent Polymarket return is followed by about a 0.26 percent Kalshi return within 30 minutes, rising to 0.37 percent in high-volume periods, and a one-standard-deviation whale order imbalance predicts 0.20 to 0.32 percent of subsequent returns. Cross-venue arbitrage in their 15-day election window totals over $130,000 and survives a 2 percent cost assumption, rising to as much as $2.2 million if the matching window is relaxed to five minutes, at correspondingly higher execution risk. The behavioral structure of the mispricing is becoming legible as well. A 2026 study of crowd wisdom across 5,456 Polymarket and Kalshi markets, using tick-level order flow and wallet-by-wallet reconstruction, finds sports markets systematically underprice mid-tier probabilities, with the inefficiency collapsing to zero precisely at the true coin-flip; finds short-duration crypto markets nearly unbiased; and, most usefully, finds no statistically significant correlation between sentiment intensity and informational edge, with whales suffering severe negative edge in attention-driven markets, consistently outperformed by small-order traders. Loud crowds are not smarter crowds, and big wallets are not smarter wallets. The oldest event markets echo the theme: a 2020 study of 136,011 soccer matches finds bookmaker odds overreact to recent extreme outcomes, and fading that overreaction is profitable even net of the bookmaker margin. The catch: the cracks are small Here the skeptic’s read matters more than usual, because the headline numbers invite the wrong conclusion. Forty million dollars of extracted arbitrage sounds like an industry, but it is one year of total extraction across an entire platform, by all participants combined. The independent follow-up work cited in our verification notes, a study of Polymarket NBA markets using full order-book snapshots, finds the opportunity is structurally bounded by liquidity, with roughly three quarters of combinatorial opportunities capped at sizes near fifteen shares and single-market episodes persisting for a median of a few seconds, confining risk-free extraction strictly to the retail scale. The arbitrage is also non-atomic: legs fill separately on an order book, so the risk-free label carries execution risk that the dollar figures do not show, a point the Probabilistic Forest authors themselves stress. Fee regimes are changing as platforms mature, and several studies above assume zero or minimal fees because that was true of their sample window; it will not stay true. And the field trial that proved cross-venue arbitrage executable did so with less than four thousand dollars, which remains the honest measure of that era’s capacity. The practical reading is that prediction-market inefficiency is real, persistent, well-documented across thirteen years and three market generations, and small. It is a good business for a bot and a poor one for a fund. Data sources and practical cautions For anyone building on this, the raw material is unusually good. Polymarket’s order flow and resolutions are on-chain and fully reconstructable, which is why the arbitrage studies could measure realized profit rather than estimate it; the Probabilistic Forest team worked from 86.6 million executed bids, and the crowd-wisdom study streams tick data and order-book snapshots from both major venues. Kalshi is a regulated exchange with a conventional API. Three cautions travel with the data. The earnings-contract comparison rests on a roughly two-month sample from a young market, so the 77 percent figure deserves a wide confidence band until it is re-measured across seasons. Prices lean optimistic in level even when they rank well, so calibrate before using them raw. And any arbitrage estimate that ignores execution risk, leg-by-leg fills, and the coming normalization of fees is an upper bound, not a business plan. Takeaways First, use the prices as inputs. Prediction markets classify earnings outcomes better than analysts, and macro event contracts carry statistically significant information for volatility in other asset classes. The forecast is the product. Second, calibrate the optimism. Markets ranked earnings outcomes well while expecting 81 percent beats against a 74 percent reality. Discrimination is strong; calibration needs an adjustment. Third, the arbitrage was real but retail-sized, in every era. Cross-venue gaps of 1 to 5 percent net in 2012, a 23-point spike on election night, $39.6 million of on-chain extraction in a year, $130,000 surviving a 2 percent cost in a 15-day window, all bounded by liquidity, execution risk, and evolving fees. Fourth, price discovery is concentrated, and depth is understated. The larger, faster venue leads by 0.26 to 0.37 percent within half an hour, and the shadow order book showed an order of magnitude more opportunity than the visible one. If you must follow one screen, follow the volume, and do not mistake the visible book for the market. Fifth, the structure of mispricing is behavioral and predictable: mid-tier probabilities underpriced, longshot asks too high, extremes overpriced, loud sentiment uninformative, and whales no wiser than small traders. That structure, not any single trade, is the durable finding.
