                                               Abstract

   We are the first to examine daily short selling activity around the expiration of initial public

   offering (IPO) share lockups. Overall, short selling is abnormally high over several weeks

   leading up to the lockup expiration date, peaks one day before the lockup expiration date, and

   declines in the following week. We find that more abnormal short selling prior to the lockup

   expiration date is associated with poorer stock returns on the lockup expiration date,

   suggesting that short sellers are informed. We also find some weak evidence that short sellers

   front-run insider sales. Unlike VC-backed IPO stocks, PE-backed IPO stocks do not

   experience a negative return or trading volume jump on the lockup expiration date.


   Key words: short sales, initial public offerings (IPOs), share lockup expiration, venture
   capital (VC), private equity (PE), buyout fund, insider sales

1. Introduction

     A typical initial public offering (IPO) sells about one third of the outstanding shares to the

general public, leaving the rest of the shares being mostly locked up for a few months after the

IPO. IPO share lockup expiration dates represent the first time when the shares that are locked up

can be sold on the market, and consequently, the public float may be increased significantly.

IPO share lockup expiration dates are usually associated with more active trading and more

volatile returns. While the share lockup expiration date is pre-scheduled and trading activity on

this day is widely watched by market participants, it is not known beforehand how many shares

will be sold by insiders, and more importantly, how the stock price behaves on the lockup

expiration date.



                                                2
     Although the literature documents an average abnormal return of -1.5% on the lockup

expiration date (Field and Hanka, 2001), returns vary significantly from firm to firm on this

widely-watched date. For example, Odimo Inc, a venture capital (VC)-backed online retailer of

watches, jewelry, and luxury goods that went public in February 2005, saw its share price tumble

33% on its lockup expiration date. In contrast, shares of PokerTek Inc., a maker of electronic

poker tables that went public in October 2005, surged 14% on its lockup expiration date. In our

opening example above, VC-backed Yelp Inc.’ stock soared nearly 23% on August 29, 2012, its

lockup expiration date, which leads to the comment quoted in this Wall Street Journal article that

“The lesson for all those people that got short in anticipation of the lockup [ending] is there is no

such thing as a sure thing.” The significant cross-sectional variation of stock returns on lockup

expiration dates creates valuable trading opportunities and often results in a huge jump in the

trading volume upon the expiration of share lockups.

     To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to examine daily short selling activity around

the expiration of IPO share lockups. Several questions are interesting to answer using daily short

sale data. First, does short selling increase in the days leading up to the lockup expiration date? If

short sellers expect price decline on the lockup expiration date on average, they may increase

short selling right before the lockup expiration date. In addition, the previous literature

documents that the abnormal return around the lockup expiration date is more negative for VC-

backed IPOs than for non-VC-backed IPOs (see Field and Hanka, 2001, Bradley et al., 2001).

Given this finding, does short selling increase more for VC-backed IPO stocks than for non-VC-

backed IPO stocks before the lockup expiration date? Furthermore, buyout fund-backed IPOs

represent the largest group of IPOs (40%) in our sample, and the average firm size for a buyout

      Second, is short selling before the lockup expiration date informative about the abnormal

return on the lockup expiration date. Third, we examine whether short selling is related to insider

and declines in the following week. Short selling before the lockup expiration date is heavier for

buyout fund-backed IPO stocks than for other stocks.3 This finding might reflect short sellers’

expectation about heavier insider selling upon lockup expiration for buyout fund-backed IPO

stocks. This expectation is consistent with a popular view that buyout funds are short-term

oriented and do not maintain their ownership of companies for a sustained time (e.g., Kosman

2009). However, unlike for VC-backed IPO stocks, we do not find a negative return or a jump in

     Second, our cross-sectional regression results suggest that short selling over several days

prior to the lockup expiration date is informative about the stock return on the lockup expiration

date. In particular, a one standard deviation increase in the market-adjusted return on the lockup

expiration date (4.5%) is associated with a 10.8% decrease in the abnormal short selling during

five trading days prior to the lockup expiration date, where the abnormal short selling is

computed as the percentage deviation of the short-to-trading volume ratio from the individual

stock’s average short-to-trading volume ratio over the benchmark period. Given that the average

abnormal short selling is 39% during the five trading days prior to the lockup expiration date, a

10.8% decrease represents 27% of the average abnormal short selling. Furthermore, we confirm

that the abnormal return on the lockup expiration date is more negative for VC-backed IPO

stocks than for non-VC-backed IPO stocks, consistent with the literature; meanwhile, we do not

find the short selling prior to the lockup expiration date to be more informative about the stock

return on the lockup expiration day for VC-backed IPO stocks than for non-VC-backed IPO

stocks. We discuss reasons for this finding.

     Third, we find some weak evidence that short sellers front run insider sales. We find that

10% of the IPO firms have insider sales during the six-day period following the expiration of

share lockups, and insider selling ranges from zero to 24% of the outstanding shares. Note that

we can only estimate a lower bound of the true insider selling, because for buyout fund-backed

and VC-backed firms, insider selling may take the form of limited partnership fund distribution,

which is typically not captured by insider filing data. The inability of insider filing data to



                                                5
capture the insider selling in disguise (i.e., fund distribution) prevents us from finding stronger

evidence. We find that insider selling during the post-lockup expiration six-day period can partly

explain short selling prior to the lockup expiration date. In particular, a one standard deviation

increase in insider selling during the six days following the lockup expiration (1.71%) is

associated with a 0.07% increase in the abnormal short selling during the five days prior to the

lockup expiration date. Furthermore, after controlling for insider selling, our finding that short

selling prior to the lockup expiration date is informative about the stock return on the lockup

expiration date remains robust, which suggests that short sellers’ private information is more

than the reported insider selling around lockup expiration dates.

      Although our primary focus is short selling around the expiration of IPO share lockups, we

also find that the SDC new issues database reports a lockup expiration date that is one day earlier

than the actual lockup expiration date for 78% of the IPOs in our sample. We conjecture that this

one day error may contribute, in part, to the finding in the literature that trading volume jumps

one day after the lockup expiration date rather than on the lockup expiration date itself. For

example, see Field and Hanka (2001, Figure 3), Bradley, et. al (2001, Figure V), Brav and

Gompers (2003, Figure 2), and Cao, Field, and Hanka (2004, Figure 1), among others, for the

finding that trading volume jumps one day after the lockup expiration date.

        Our study makes three contributions. First, we fill a gap in the IPO lockup expiration

literature by providing empirical evidence on how short sellers trade around the lockup

expiration date on a daily basis. IPO share lockup is among the most-widely watched features of

IPOs. However, the existing literature on IPO lockup expiration primarily focuses on stock

returns and aggregate trading activity around lockup expiration dates.4 Due to data availability,

2. Related Literature and Hypothesis Development

      An extensive body of research has examined short selling.6 However, research on short

selling in IPO stocks is scant. A notable exception is Edwards and Hanley (2010), which focuses

on short selling in the immediate aftermarket and documents active short selling on the first

trading day for IPO stocks.7 However, Edwards and Hanley (2010) do not examine short selling

around the expiration of IPO share lockups.

      IPO share lockup expiration represents a unique setting to study short selling, because IPO

stocks are new to the public and are less likely to be associated with traded options. For IPO

stocks without traded options, short selling becomes the only way for pessimistic investors to

express their view in the stock market.

      It is an empirical question whether short sellers try to take advantage of the expected price

decline on the lockup expiration date. On the one hand, short selling may increase before the

lockup expiration date because the literature has documented significantly negative stock returns

around the share lockup expiration date. For example, using IPOs during the period 1988-1997,

Field and Hanka (2001) find a statistically significant three-day abnormal return of -1.5% around

share lockup expirations, and the effect is roughly three times larger in venture-backed firms

compared to non-venture-backed firms. Bradley et al. (2001) further find that the largest losses

occur for high-tech firms and firms with the greatest post-IPO stock price increases and most

reputable underwriters. However, on the other hand, Ofek and Richardson (2000) and Field and

Hanka (2001) show that paying the bid/ask spread eliminates much or all of the profit. Therefore,

3.4. Measure of short selling

     Our daily short-sale data are from the American Stock Exchange (AMEX), Archipelago,

Boston Stock Exchange, Chicago Stock Exchange, National Association of Securities Dealers,

National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotations (NASDAQ), National Stock

Exchange (formerly known as the Cincinnati Stock Exchange), Philadelphia Stock Exchange,
                                                 15
and the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). All of the above self-regulatory organizations

(SROs) made tick data on short sales publicly available starting on January 2, 2005 as a result of

the SEC’s Regulation SHO adopted in 2004. While the short sale data for NYSE is available

through the TAQ database, all other SROs make short sale data available only on their own

websites. The ending date for the available Regulation SHO data varies from May to August of

2007 across the various SROs. We therefore select a sample period during which short sale data

are available for all the SROs (i.e., January 2005 to May 2007). The SHO dataset also includes

an indicator variable denoting whether a transaction is exempt from price test rules. Since

exempt transactions apply to special cases such as trades related to “bona fide” market making,

we exclude such transactions from our main empirical analysis.19

      We aggregate the intra-day transaction data on short sale on a daily basis. We obtain stock

4.2. Are short sellers particularly informed about the lockup expiration date return?

      Given our finding that short selling is informative about the stock return on the lockup

expiration date, next we examine whether short sellers are similarly informed about the stock

return on a few days around the lockup expiration date. Answering this question can help us

better understand short sellers’ information advantage over other traders.

      Engelberg, Reed, and Ringgenberg (2012) find that the negative relation between short

sales and future returns is mostly driven by news days, especially days with negative news. They

also find that short sellers have superior ability to analyze publicly available information,

particularly for news categories such as earnings, earnings projections, high yield issuers, new

products and services, stock ownership, analyst comments and ratings, bond ratings and

comments, divestitures or asset sales, and lawsuits. Table 9 in their paper reports all the 39 news

categories that they examine. They do not examine IPO lockup expirations.

      Compared with some of the news categories examined in Engelberg, Reed, and

Ringgenberg (2012), IPO share lockup expiration has some similarities and differences. For

example, similar to earnings announcements, share lockup expiration dates are also pre-



                                                23
scheduled. However, different from many other news categories, typically no news is publicly

announced on the lockup expiration date.

     We run the regression in Equation (3) for each of the two days before and two days after

the lockup expiration date. In other words, in each regression, the dependent variable is the

average short selling over five days before the event date, and the key independent variable is the

market-adjusted return on the event day. We set the event day to be each of the days around the

lockup expiration date. We do not find any evidence that short sellers are informed about the

stock return on any of the two days before or after the lockup expiration date. In other words, the

only event date for which there is a significantly negative relation between the pre-event date

short selling and event date stock return is the lockup expiration date. For brevity, Table 5

reports the results for days -1, 0 and 1 around the lockup expiration date. While the coefficient

on the market-adjusted return is statistically significant for the lockup expiration date, it is not

statistically significant for the two days around the lockup expiration date. This finding is

interesting given that the average market-adjusted return is not more negative on the lockup

expiration date than on the two days around the lockup expiration date. However, the standard

deviation of the market-adjusted return is larger for the lockup expiration date than the two days

around the lockup expiration date. We conclude that short sellers are particularly informed about

the stock return on the lockup expiration date.

4.3. Short selling by market makers

     Our study focuses on short sale transactions made by non-market makers. However, it is an

empirical question whether market makers make more informed short sales before the lockup

expiration date than non-market makers. While Engelberg, Reed, and Ringgenberg (2012) find

that short sales made by market-makers are not informative about future returns in general, this

may not hold true for lockup expirations. Because the sudden increase in share supply on the
                                                  24
lockup expiration date is expected to move the stock price, the slope of the demand curve for the

IPO stock is expected to affect the stock return on the lockup expiration date. It is possible that

market-makers are more informed about the slope of the demand curve than other traders.

     Using all the short sale transactions made by both market makers and non-market makers,

we replicate the regression analysis in Table 4. Table 6 reports the results. While the coefficient

on the market-adjusted return on the lockup expiration date is still negative and statistically

significant, ranging from 1.56 to 1.63, the magnitude of the coefficient is about 32% smaller

than the coefficient in Panel A of Table 4. The finding suggests that market making activities are

less informative about future returns.

4.4. Insider selling around the lockup expiration date

     In this section, we explore the extent to which short selling is related to insider selling

around the lockup expiration date. Our objective is to understand if the informed short selling

prior to the lockup expiration date, as we document before, can be explained by the insider sales

prior to the lockup expiration date as well as the insider sales shortly after the lockup period

expires.

     We obtain insider trading data from the Thomson Reuters Insider Filing Data Files.

Corporate insiders are defined broadly to include those that have "access to non-public, material,

insider information" and these insiders are required to file SEC form 3, 4, and 5 when they trade

in their companies stock (Thomson Reuters Insider Filing Data Manual).

     However, insider sales in VC-backed IPO stocks or PE-backed IPO stocks may not be

completely captured by the reported insider sales for the following reason. VC and PE funds are

typically organized as limited partnerships. They can distribute either cash or portfolio firms’

stocks to their limited partners, who are investors of the VC or PE funds. Gompers and Lerner



                                                25
(1998) discuss several reasons for why VC funds prefer share distribution over cash distribution.

Their footnote 3 provides a detailed explanation about VC distributions. However, share

distribution makes it difficult for a researcher to identify the exit of VC funds from their

investment, because VC fund investors’ sale of stock are not required to be filed with the SEC

unless the investor owns at least 10% of the outstanding shares of the IPO firm or is affiliated

with the IPO firm. With the caveat of the data in mind, we compute insider selling using the

Thomson Reuters Insider Filing Data Files.

     Table 7 shows descriptive statistics for insider sales around the lockup expiration date. We

have two observations. First, as shown in Panel A, insider selling is less active prior to the

lockup expiration date than after the lockup expiration date, which is not surprising given that

our study focuses on the biggest share lockup. Recall our discussion in Section 3.3 that insiders

can sell only an average of 3% of the outstanding shares prior to the expiration of the biggest

share lockup. Second, insider selling shortly after the expiration of lockup period does not seem

to be active in general. Specifically, about 4.2% of the IPOs have insider selling on the lockup

expiration date, and about 7.5% of the IPOs have insider selling within 5 trading days following

the lockup expiration date.

     Panel B of Table 7 shows insider selling during days [0, 5] relative to the lockup expiration

date for VC-backed, PE-backed, and non-sponsored IPOs. The average ratio of the number of

shares sold by insiders to the number of shares outstanding is 0.10% for VC-backed IPOs, 0.35%

for PE-backed IPOs, and 0.11% for non-sponsored IPOs. Note that the seemingly inactive insider

selling for VC-backed or PE-backed IPOs may be due to the fact that VC and PE fund

distribution of shares to partners is not required to be reported to the SEC as insider sales. We

acknowledge that this is the major drawback of our insider sales data. If VC and PE fund



                                               26
distribution data were available, we would be able to provide a complete picture of insider

selling around lockup expirations.

      With the caveat about our data limitation in mind, we test whether short selling prior to the

lockup expiration date can be explained by insider selling around the lockup expiration date. In

particular, we measure insider selling shortly before and after the five day window over which

our main short selling variable is computed. Because our short selling measure is computed over

trading days [-5,-1] relative to the lockup expiration date, we first compute insider selling prior

to this five-day period. Specifically, we compute insider selling over trading days [-14, -6]

relative to the lockup expiration date. We then compute insider selling over days [0, 5] relative to

the lockup expiration date. Our results are robust to measuring insider selling on the lockup

expiration date only rather than over days [0, 5]. We measure insider selling as the aggregate

shares sold by insiders divided by the total number of shares outstanding. We add these two

insider selling variables into the regression model in Equation (3). If short sellers front run

insider sales, then we would see short selling prior to the lockup expiration date increases more

for IPOs with more insider selling shortly after the lockup period expires, even though the post-

lockup expiration insider sales are not publicly known before lockup expirations.

of insider selling after lockup expiration is not publicly known before the lockup expiration date,

4. Conclusion

     In this study, we examine daily short selling activity around the expiration of IPO share

lockups. Overall, short selling is abnormally high over several weeks leading up to the lockup

expiration date, peaks one day before the lockup expiration date, and declines in the following

week. We find informed abnormal short selling prior to the lockup expiration date. We also find

some weak evidence that short sellers front-run insider sales.

     The past ten years have witnessed an increasing number of PE-backed IPOs in the U.S.

market. The increase is particularly heightened during our sample period of 2005-2006. Indeed,

PE-backed IPOs account for 40% of our sample and VC-backed IPOs account for 37% of our

sample. Consistent with a popular view that PE funds aim for short-term profits and thus might

cash out soon after bringing a firm public, we find that short selling prior to the lockup expiration

date is heavier for PE-backed IPO stocks than for other IPO stocks. However, we find neither a

negative return nor a jump in trading volume on the lockup expiration date for PE-backed IPO

stocks.

     Due to data limitation, we are unable to answer several interesting questions. For example,

why aren’t the stock returns negative on the lockup expiration date for PE-backed IPO stocks as

for VC-backed IPO stocks? Why doesn’t the trading volume jump on the lockup expiration date

for PE-backed IPO stocks as much as for VC-backed IPO stocks? How long do private equity

firms maintain their investment in their portfolio firms after the firm goes public? Given that

private equity firms play an increasingly important role in supporting private and young firms, it

is important for future research to shed light on these issues.

preSEO is an indicator variable that equals one if the firm made seasoned offers before the unlock date, and zero
otherwise. Earning is an indicator variable that equals one if earnings is announced within 3 days of the unlock date,
and zero otherwise. Hold2 is 13f holdings prior to the unlock date. Underp is underpricing. Secondary is the ratio of
shareholders’ shares to the total shares offered in the IPO. Options is an indicator variable that equals one if the IPO
stock has traded options before the lockup expiration date, and zero otherwise. Accounting variables are obtained
from unrestated quarterly Compustat file as of the quarter immediately prior to the lockup expiration date. Sales is
quarterly sales in $million (SALEQ). ROA is income before extraordinary items scaled by total assets (IBQ/ATQ).
Debt/Assets is total liability scaled by total assets (LTQ/ATQ). Cash/Assets is cash and cash equivalent scaled by
total assets (CHECHQ/ATQ). Net PPE/Assets is net property, plant, and equipment scaled by total assets
(PPENTQ/ATQ). CapEx/Assets is capital expenditures scaled by total assets (CAPXQ/ATQ). Panel A reports
summary statistics for the full sample. In Panel B, we break down the sample into VC-backed, PE-backed, and non-
