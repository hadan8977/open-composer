The idea An ETF and the stocks inside it are claims on (almost) the same cash flows, so their prices are mechanically tied together. But they do not process information at the same speed. The ETF is extremely liquid and cheap to trade. When a macro or sector shock hits, market makers and systematic traders reprice the ETF in seconds. The individual constituents are slower . Some are less liquid, some are followed by fewer traders, and the basket as a whole takes time to fully reflect the same news. That difference in speed creates a predictable lead–lag : the ETF's recent move carries information about where its constituents are heading. There are two opposite ways this can play out over the next bar, and which one dominates depends entirely on the time horizon you trade : Regime Story Where it lives Momentum / diffusion Slow stocks under-react and then catch up in the ETF's direction. Intraday (seconds → minutes). The classic "ETF leads, stocks follow." Reversion / convergence The liquid ETF overshoots on the shock, then the basket mean-reverts back toward its ETF-implied fair value. Daily close-to-close, where the fast diffusion has already been arbitraged away. This project builds the full statistical machinery to measure the lead–lag, then trades it, and is honest about which regime the data actually supports at the frequency you can get for free. The signal, precisely For each constituent i we estimate a rolling beta to the ETF ( Cov(rᵢ, r_ETF) / Var(r_ETF) ). The ETF's recent move implies a "fair" move for each stock: expectedᵢ,ₜ = betaᵢ,ₜ · (recent ETF return) predᵢ,ₜ = sign · expectedᵢ,ₜ # sign = +1 momentum, −1 reversion We only deploy risk on macro-shock bars — bars whose |ETF return| is in the top tercile of a trailing (causal, no look-ahead) window — because that is when there is something to diffuse. On calm bars we sit flat, which also slashes turnover and trading costs. Positions are signal-weighted and gross-normalised, then held for the next bar (a weight formed at the close of bar t earns the return of bar t+1 — no look-ahead). Transaction costs are charged on turnover. What the data says On free daily data for a liquid mega-cap universe (the Technology Select Sector SPDR, XLK , leading 24 of its constituents, 2015→present): The lead–lag is statistically real. The ETF Granger-causes 22 of 24 constituents at p < 0.05. The diffusion is sub-daily. By the close, the momentum version is gone — the reversion version is what pays. Day t ETF moves are negatively correlated with day t+1 basket returns (≈ −0.14, a large daily figure). Macro-shock gating helps , exactly as the thesis predicts: restricting the book to large-ETF-move bars raises the reversion Sharpe from ≈ 0.9 to ≈ 1.3 and cuts turnover. The backtest therefore ships with the ETF-implied reversion book as the default, and runs the momentum book alongside it as an honest counter-example (it loses money here, by construction — that is the finding). Want the momentum/diffusion edge to pay? It lives intraday. Yahoo gives ~730 days of free 1-hour and ~60 days of 1-minute bars — run --interval 1h to test the higher-frequency regime. For true minute-level multi-year data you need a paid feed (Polygon, Databento, etc.); the code is frequency-agnostic. The three econometric tests The strategy stands on three classic tools, each answering a different question ( src/stats_tests.py ): Granger causality — Do past ETF returns help predict a stock's return beyond the stock's own past? An F-test per constituent over lags 1…5. Small p ⇒ the ETF leads that stock. VAR impulse response — If the ETF gets a one-standard-deviation surprise today, how does the constituent basket respond over the next bars? This traces the shape and speed of information diffusion. Rolling cointegration — Do the ETF and basket prices stay tethered, and is that tether stable through time? A rolling Engle–Granger test plus the rolling hedge ratio. A persistently low p -value is the mean-reversion anchor that makes the convergence trade safe to hold. Useful flags python main.py --interval 1h # higher-frequency regime (~2y free history) python main.py --leader XLF # swap the sector ETF (financials, energy XLE, …) python main.py --start 2018-01-01 # restrict the sample period python main.py --refresh # ignore the cache and re-download Everything else — the universe, beta window, shock threshold, transaction costs, long/short direction, dollar-neutral vs directional — lives in config.py and is documented inline. How the backtest avoids fooling you Quant backtests lie easily. This one is built to make that hard: No look-ahead. Signals at bar t use only data through t ; positions earn t+1 returns (weights are shifted forward before multiplying by returns). Causal shock gate. The "large move" threshold is a trailing rolling quantile, never the full-sample distribution. Costs are real. Turnover is charged in basis points every bar. The market exposure is reported. Because the default book is directional, the metrics include its realised market beta and average net exposure so you can see how much of the return is alpha vs. just being short the market. An honest counter-example runs every time. The opposite-sign book is backtested side-by-side; if the "edge" were a sign error or an artifact, the mirror image would look just as good. It doesn't. Interpreting the results The default daily run produces (numbers move with the data): A reversion book with a Sharpe around 1.2–1.3 , low market beta, and a drawdown profile far gentler than buy-and-hold. A momentum book that is its near-perfect, money-losing mirror. Granger evidence that the ETF genuinely leads its constituents. The honest takeaway is not "free money." It is that the information-diffusion lead–lag is real and measurable , that on liquid names the fast (momentum) leg is arbitraged away by the daily close, and that the residual, tradable structure at that horizon is ETF-implied mean-reversion , strongest right after macro shocks — exactly where the economics says it should be. Extending it Go intraday ( --interval 1h ) to hunt the momentum/diffusion leg directly. Add less-liquid constituents — the lead–lag is mechanically stronger for smaller, slower names (Hou, 2007). Build the reverse leg : when the constituent basket moves but the ETF hasn't caught up, trade the ETF (the framework already separates the two sides). Swap the universe to any sector ETF and its holdings via config.py . Data & dependencies All data is pulled from Yahoo Finance via yfinance — free, no API key. Statistics use statsmodels ; plotting uses matplotlib . For more free quant tools see awesome-quant . Disclaimer This is a research and educational project. It is not investment advice. Backtested performance is not indicative of future results, ignores many real-world frictions (borrow costs, market impact, financing, slippage beyond the modelled spread), and should not be traded with real capital as-is.
