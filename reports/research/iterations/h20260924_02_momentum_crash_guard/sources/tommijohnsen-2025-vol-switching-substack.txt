Is managing momentum crash risk with volatility switching the best way to go? 
The answer: YES.
Tommi Johnsen, PhD
Aug 14, 2025
4
Share
Switching strategies entirely, during high volatility is more effective than simply scaling down momentum’s risk. Simply put: when the market gets turbulent, this method changes strategy rather than just scaling it down.
Research published in the Journal of Asset Management in 2024, by Butt, Kolari and Sadaqat found the switching approach provided a superior balance of return and risk. It essentially solves the “momentum crash” problem that has plagued momentum investors.

However, the switching approach is pretty radical.
It entails entirely switching from momentum to reversal during high-volatility periods. A reversal strategy is essentially the opposite of momentum. It 
buys recent losers and shorts recent winners
 by focusing on very short lookback like 1 month.
The research shows that when volatility is high, momentum tends to falter and actually 
reverses
 (yesterday’s losers bounce back, yesterday’s winners bounce down), so a contrarian stance wins. One approach to implementation is to check the market’s volatility at each month-end relative to history.
If the current month’s market volatility is below the 75th percentile of the past 5 years’ volatility then momentum is implemented. Otherwise the reversal strategy is implemented”. That is to say, if volatility is in a “normal” or low regime, then stay with momentum. If volatility spikes into the top quartile of its 5-year range, pivot to a reversal for the next month. When triggered, the strategy 
fully allocates to reversal
 (long last-month losers, short winners) instead of momentum.
Another way to manage crash risk is volatility scaling
,
Volatility scaling means adjusting the momentum portfolio’s size according to recent volatility. When volatility is high, you scale down, that is take a smaller position. When volatility is low, you scale up (take a larger position) to target a roughly constant level of strategy risk.
In practice, this often involves computing a trailing volatility over the last 6 months or 126 trading days, and using it as an inverse factor for position sizing. For example, Pedro Barroso and Pedro Santa-Clara (2015) proposed a well-known method to target a fixed 12% annual volatility for momentum by cutting exposure when momentum’s own volatility is elevated and increasing it when momentum is steadier. The idea is that by keeping volatility in check, one can avoid the worst crashes without completely abandoning the strategy.
While beneficial and simpler to implement within a momentum mandate, scaling does not fully address tail-risk. It appears to be a partial remedy that still leaves some performance on the table during recovery phases. The goal is a smoother ride: momentum’s strong returns but tempered volatility. In a nutshell:
Momentum investing (buying recent winners and shorting losers) can generate strong returns, but it is prone to occasional dramatic crashes during turbulent markets. The debate is how best to manage this risk: should one scale down the momentum exposure when volatility is high, or switch entirely to a different strategy (reversing away from momentum) during those periods?
In plain English, the question is whether to “turn down the volume” on momentum or “change the channel” when markets get wild. According to Butt, Kolari, and Sadaqat (2024), momentum profits indeed 
“collapse and reversal occurs when preceding market volatility is relatively high”
. They explore two approaches to handle this: reducing momentum position sizes based on volatility, versus flipping to a reversal strategy in high-vol regimes. Their findings indicate the switching approach can eliminate momentum crash losses and improve performance relative to scaled-down momentum. The trade-off is that switching is a more drastic change and requires comfort with a dynamic strategy, whereas volatility scaling is a more incremental adjustment.
